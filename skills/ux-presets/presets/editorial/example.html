The last keepers of the light

Norway automated its final manned lighthouse in 2002. Three of the people who lived in them still go back every winter.

Ingrid SolbergPhotographs by Tomas Ek · 14 min read · 6 November 2026

The boat from Bodø takes two hours when the sea is kind. Arne Pedersen has made the trip forty-one times since he handed over the keys, and he still stands at the rail for the last mile, watching the tower lift out of the swell as if it might not be there.

He kept Skomvær light for nineteen years. He knows the weight of the lens, the sound of the clockwork, the number of steps from the kitchen to the gallery. What he did not know, he says, was how quiet the island would be once the light ran itself.

IThe handover

Automation came slowly and then all at once. Between 1970 and 2002 the Norwegian Coastal Administration replaced keepers at every station, first with timers, then with sensors and a radio link to the mainland.

  1. 1970The first stations get electric lamps and timers.
  2. 1987Skomvær's keepers drop from three to one.
  3. 2002The last keeper leaves Ytre Møkkalasset.
“You do not keep a light. It keeps you, and then one day it stops needing you.”Arne Pedersen, keeper of Skomvær, 1983–2002

IIGoing back

Every February the three of them book the same week. They repaint what the salt has taken, check the logbook they still keep by hand, and eat at the table where the radio used to sit.

Nobody asked them to. The administration pays for the fuel, and the island has no other visitors in winter.
